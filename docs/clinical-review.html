Kindrest — Request for Clinical Review

A daily support app for mothers. Live, in use, and never reviewed by anyone clinical.

Jheanelle Howell, Founder  ·  jheanelle@kindrest.co  ·  kindrest.co  ·  August 2026

1. What Kindrest is

A free app for mothers in the years after birth — not pregnancy, not delivery, but the long stretch afterwards where structured support has usually stopped.

It asks one question a day, answerable in about fifteen seconds. She writes a sentence or taps “not today.” From there she can take a two-minute check-in that returns a few small practices, mark items on a “Rest Card” that were already true of her day, or write privately in a journal.

No streaks, no scores, no way to fall behind. A points-and-rewards system was built and deliberately removed before launch.

Kindrest does not claim to be therapy, treatment, or clinical care, and is not staffed by clinicians. It is a daily layer between appointments — for women who, in most cases, are not in treatment at all. It was built by one person with no clinical supervision, which is exactly why I'm asking.

The request: tell me where this is clinically unsound, ethically questionable, or likely to land badly on someone’s worst day. Everything below is the actual content — you can review it on paper without opening the app.

2. The safety net — two tiers — highest priority

Everything she writes — the daily question, check-in reflections, journal entries — is scanned for language suggesting she may be in danger, or carrying something heavier than Kindrest is built for. It is keyword matching, not a clinical screening instrument. Nothing she writes is ever blocked, deleted, or hidden from her.

There are two levels, and the same sentence never produces both. Tier 1 is checked first and wins outright. The tiers exist because everything used to fire the same alarm — and showing a mother the full 988 card for “I feel numb” teaches her to dismiss it, which costs the one moment it genuinely mattered.

Tier 1 — Danger. Sixteen triggers, full crisis card.

  1. “kill myself” / “killing myself”
  2. any word containing “suicid–”
  3. “end my life” / “ending my life”
  4. “end it all”
  5. “don’t want to be here”
  6. “want to die” / “wanted to die”
  7. “wish I was dead” / “wish I were dead”
  8. “better off without me”
  9. “no reason to live”
  10. “hurt myself” / “hurting myself”
  11. “harm myself” / “harming myself”
  12. “self-harm” / “self harm”
  13. “hurt my baby” / “hurt the baby”
  14. “harm my baby” / “harm the baby”
  15. “hearing voices”
  16. “scared I’ll hurt…”

Tier 1 response, word for word

You don’t have to carry this alone right now.

What you wrote matters, and it sounds like this is more than Kindrest is built for. Please reach out to someone who can help right now:

Suicide & Crisis Lifeline: Call or text 988
Postpartum Support International: 1-800-944-4773
Crisis Text Line: Text HOME to 741741

If you or your baby are in immediate danger, please call 911.

Tier 2 — Distress. Eighteen triggers, a gentle card and a warmline. No 988, no 911.

  1. Intrusive: “intrusive thought”
  2. “can’t stop thinking”
  3. “thoughts I can’t control”
  4. “what if I dropped / hurt / lost”
  5. Fear of self: “scared of myself”
  6. “don’t trust myself”
  7. “afraid of what I…”
  8. “I’m not safe”
  9. Escape: “want to disappear / run away”
  10. “they’d be better off” (without “me”)
  11. “no one would notice”
  12. Numbness: “feel nothing” / “feel numb”
  13. “can’t feel anything”
  14. “watching myself from…”
  15. “not really here”
  16. The baby: “hate / resent my baby”
  17. “don’t love / feel connected to the baby”
  18. “regret having…”

Tier 2 response, word for word

That sounds heavy.

What you wrote is yours and it stays here. If any of it has been sitting on you, there are people who talk to mothers about exactly this — no crisis required, and it’s free.

Postpartum Support International · 1-800-944-4773 — call or text. They’ll talk about anything; you don’t have to be in crisis.

Deliberately excluded from both tiers: ordinary venting — “I’m exhausted”, “I feel like a bad mother”, “I can’t do this anymore”. These are close to universal postpartum, and including them would fire on nearly everyone and teach her to ignore the card. That is a judgement call, and the one I would most like you to challenge.

2a. What is Tier 1 missing — particularly postpartum-specific presentations such as early psychosis, and the ways these appear in ordinary language rather than clinical phrasing?

2b. Is anything in Tier 2 actually Tier 1, or the reverse?

2c. Was excluding ordinary venting right, or is that exactly the moment to say something?

2d. Are those the right resources, in the right order, for each tier?

2e. Is a warmline the right Tier 2 response — or should it do more, or less?

3. The day she has nothing good to say — highest priority

The daily question asks her to notice something small and positive. On a hard day that could land badly, so she can tap “not today” and is asked one thing:

The fork, word for word

Option 1 — “A calm or plain day. Nothing’s wrong.”

Option 2 — “Today’s asking a lot of me.”

Choosing the second returns: “Thank you for being honest.” then an offer — “Check in with me — two minutes. No performing, no fixing.”

Separately, if she reports three or more hard days within any fourteen, a card appears on her home screen. It can be dismissed and does not nag.

The nudge, word for word

These past couple of weeks have been heavy.

You’ve shown up on some hard days lately — that takes something. You don’t have to carry it alone. Whenever you want, someone’s here.

Postpartum Support International: 1-800-944-4773  ·  988

3a. Is that fork sufficient, and is the wording right?

3b. Is three hard days in fourteen a defensible threshold? I chose it by instinct, not evidence.

3c. Does a positive-affect prompt risk harm for someone depressed, and does the fork adequately mitigate that?

3d. Should a stretch of heavy days do more than show a card?

4. The eleven daily questions

One per day on rotation, the same for every user, drawn from work on glimmers and polyvagal theory (Deb Dana), matrescence (Aurélie Athan), and savoring (Hanson; Fredrickson).

  1. When did you last feel most like yourself — not a role, just you?
  2. What’s something you did recently that felt unmistakably like you?
  3. When did you last notice your shoulders drop, even for a second?
  4. What tiny thing made you smile today when you weren’t expecting to?
  5. What’s a small pleasure that’s just yours — tied to no one else’s needs?
  6. When did you last feel genuinely with someone — a look, a laugh, a small understanding?
  7. What’s something recently that made you feel alive, not just functional?
  8. When did you last catch a glimpse of the woman you were before all this — and feel glad she’s still here?
  9. What’s a moment from today you’d want to keep, even a plain one?
  10. What did your body quietly enjoy today — warmth, rest, a good stretch, a first sip?
  11. What are you quietly looking forward to, however small?

4a. Is that theoretical grounding sound, or have I misapplied it?

4b. Is any single question a poor idea for someone grieving, in a difficult relationship, or unwell? Number 8 is the one I am least sure of.

5. The forty-six practices she may be offered

A check-in returns a small number of these, matched to her stated mood and available time. All low-effort by design — nothing requiring money, childcare, or leaving the house. Grouped by the kind of regulation each tends toward.

Physical (12)

  • Move your body in a way that feels celebratory
  • Close your eyes for 90 seconds
  • Take a 5-minute walk — no destination
  • Shake out your hands and roll your neck
  • Let your body be horizontal
  • Feel the ground hold you
  • Do one thing slowly on purpose
  • Notice what your body feels like when things are okay
  • Drink a full glass of water
  • Stretch your shoulders
  • Take 3 slow breaths
  • Stand in sunlight briefly

Emotional (8)

  • Put one hand on your chest
  • Pause and savor this moment
  • Check in: what emotion is actually here right now?
  • Express gratitude out loud
  • Write one compassionate sentence to yourself
  • Write: Right now I wish someone knew…
  • Sit quietly before bed
  • Let yourself feel good without qualifying it

Mental (8)

  • Say out loud: “I only have to handle right now”
  • Voice memo: name what feels heavy
  • Close every tab you are not using
  • Get one thing out of your head and onto paper
  • Colour something — no goal, no rules
  • Spend 5 minutes colouring
  • Write your three non-negotiables for tomorrow
  • Identify what made today lighter

Sensory (7)

  • Lower the noise
  • Put phone away for 10 minutes
  • Put on music that’s just for you — not the kids
  • Create a small ritual that marks this as a good day
  • Do something that feels indulgent but is actually just normal
  • Make something warm to drink and actually sit down with it
  • Change one thing about your physical environment

Relational (6)

  • Send a quick “thinking of you” to someone
  • Read a message from someone who loves you
  • Text someone safe
  • Share one real thing with someone safe today
  • Make a plan with someone you love — not because anything is wrong
  • Text someone: I am having a hard day

Identity (5)

  • Record a voice memo to yourself
  • Name one thing that is still yours
  • Celebrate one small win
  • Spend 3 minutes with something that made you you
  • Plan something to look forward to — and put it in the calendar

5a. Is anything here contraindicated for a specific presentation — trauma, dissociation, disordered eating, OCD?

5b. Is “low-effort self-regulation” the right frame at all for someone who in fact needs treatment?

5c. Anything you would remove outright?

6. Boundaries, and messages from other people

Kindrest states plainly that it is not therapy and not a replacement for clinical care. It also includes a feature called Love Notes: she shares a private link, and people she chooses can leave a short message that appears on her home screen. They need no account and can never see anything she writes.

The first note from a name she hasn’t heard from waits for her approval — she sees it in Love Notes and chooses to keep it or block the sender. Once allowed, that person’s later notes appear directly, so her mother isn’t re-approved every time. She can delete any note or close the link instantly.

6a. Is the “this is not therapy” boundary clear enough, and where might a user still mistake it for care?

6b. Is first-note approval the right safeguard, or should every note wait?

6c. Any concern with third parties sending messages to someone who may be unwell?

7. What I am not asking

  • Not asking for endorsement or to use your name. If that ever arises it will be a separate conversation, in writing.
  • Not asking for a formal report, documentation, or unpaid consulting hours.
  • Not asking about design, branding, or the business — only whether this is safe and sound.

If any of this creates a duty or a conflict for you professionally, please say so and I will withdraw the request. I would rather lose the feedback than put you in an awkward position.

8. How to reply

Section numbers are there so you can answer in shorthand — “2b: numbness belongs in Tier 1” is perfect. Email, voice note, bullet points, or a call all work. Partial answers are genuinely useful.

If you would rather see it running, it is at kindrest.co — free, about two minutes to sign up. Writing one of the phrases above into the journal will show you the matching response on a real account.

And if you think a mother you know would find this useful, you’re welcome to pass it on — only if it genuinely would help her, and only if it sits comfortably with your role.
